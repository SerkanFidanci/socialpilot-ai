# Content planner (PRD §13) — slice 2G

**Status:** implemented on `slice/2g-content-planner` · **Module:**
[`services/api/app/modules/planner/`](../../services/api/app/modules/planner/CLAUDE.md)

Slices 2A–2F can produce a content project, run it to a preview, get it approved and revise it —
but only ever because a person pressed something. This slice is the part of the product that
works while the business is asleep: a subscription turns into demand, demand turns into work, and
approved work gets a place on the calendar.

## The chain

```text
planner_subscription_items          standing demand, one row per "a Reel a day"
        │  planner.obligations.plan          (beat, hourly)
        ▼
content_obligations                 §13.1: which window, published when, generated by when
        │  planner.obligations.dispatch      (beat, 60 s) — the only step that spends credit
        ▼
content_projects                    §20's machine, slices 2A–2F, unchanged
        │  … script → voiceover → timeline → render → QC → preview → §21 approval …
        ▼
APPROVED ──▶ SCHEDULED              planner.projects.schedule (beat, 120 s)
                                    Phase 4 continues into PUBLISHING
```

## Four decisions

### 1. The planner produces demand, not content

Planning writes obligations; conversion opens projects; scheduling assigns slots. Three services,
three drains, and `ObligationPlanningService` holds neither an `EntitlementService` nor a
`ContentProjectService` — planning **cannot** spend money because it has nothing to spend it
with. The separation is the point rather than tidiness: if planning and production were one
operation, a planning mistake would burn credit directly; separated, it is a row somebody can
cancel before it costs anything.

Conversion goes through `ContentProjectService.create_project`, so the reservation is opened in
the transaction that creates the project (W20). A refusal for want of credit therefore leaves
**no project row at all** — and the obligation records why, visibly, through the API. An
obligation that could not be converted is never silently dropped.

### 2. The dependency runs one way

`planner` reads `content`; `content` does not know this module exists. That is why the project
reference lives on the obligation (`content_obligations.project_id`) and there is no
`obligation_id` on `content_projects`: a project can exist without an obligation — every project
slices 2A–2F produce does — while an obligation has at most one project. It is the same
discipline `entitlement` keeps through `ReservationSourceProbe`.

The one place the two modules meet in the schema is `content_projects.scheduled_publish_at`,
which is a plain instant. The sequencer never asks where it came from, and a project created by
hand carries one just the same.

### 3. `APPROVED --> SCHEDULED` belongs to the planner

PRD §20 draws `WAITING_APPROVAL --> SCHEDULED`. Slice 2F named the middle of that arrow
(`APPROVED`) because there was no scheduler yet; this slice completes it, and the PM decision
fixes the edge at **`APPROVED --> SCHEDULED`**. Content nobody approved cannot reach a calendar,
and every §21.1 policy still has a route — `never_within_guardrails` lands in `APPROVED` through
an actorless `auto_approved` record.

The edge is the planner's because the slot is a §13 question: a business timezone, a quiet window,
a standing demand. Answering it inside `content` would make that module depend on this one. The
sequencer claims `APPROVED` and does nothing; `waits_for_handoff` exempts it from the step timeout,
because waiting on the planner is no more a stalled job than waiting on an approver.

`approved` therefore stops being terminal, exactly as `preview_ready` stopped being terminal in
slice 2F, and migration `0019` moves the three objects that spelled the old terminal set — the
partial claim index and two check constraints — one state along.

### 4. The mix is measured, the priorities are ordered

**§13.2's ten priorities are a sequence, not a weighted score.** `rank_obligations` builds a
ten-component lexicographic key in the section's own numbering, so priority 1 can never be
outweighed by anything below it. Each component is a small **discrete bucket** rather than a
continuous measure, and that is what makes the later priorities reachable at all: a deadline
compared to the minute would be nearly unique per candidate, and priorities 3 through 9 would be
unreachable code wearing a specification.

Every candidate carries the derivation of its own position — one reason per priority, each naming
the rule and the bucket — and `GET …/planner/plan` returns exactly that. "Why is this content
next?" is answered by the function that decides, not by a second explanation written to look like
it.

**Two of the ten have a field and no rule, and say so.** §13.2/4 (past performance) needs data
Phase 5 produces; §13.2/10 (special days) requires by its own wording a verified calendar source
that has not been chosen, and a hand-written holiday list would be the invented fact this codebase
refuses everywhere else. Both inputs are carried on `RankContext` and read by nothing, both
contribute a constant, and a unit test proves that setting either changes no order anywhere.

**§13.3's mix is measured and reported, never enforced.** `measure_mix` has no failure mode, and
the only thing that reads a deviation is priority 3, which reorders. Nothing anywhere refuses an
obligation because a category is over its share — a hard quota would punish precisely the business
§13.2/1 says to serve first, the one running a campaign. The integration suite pins that: a tenant
whose campaign share is far over target still gets its campaign obligation converted first.

## The clock

`planned_publish_at` is meaningful in the tenant's zone and stored in UTC (AGENTS.md). Every
boundary is therefore computed from a **local calendar date** and converted once, never by adding
twenty-four hours to an instant:

- A daily period runs local midnight to local midnight, so it is 23, 24 or 25 hours long depending
  on the date. A weekly period starts on the local Monday.
- The publish slot is the tenant's preferred local minute on the period's local day.
- `generation_deadline_at` is that minus the item's lead time, which is what spreads production
  into the night queue rather than onto the publish hour (K5, PRD §13.1).

Quiet hours (§13.2/8) **shift, never cancel**: a slot inside the window is pushed to the instant
the window ends, in local terms, and a slot outside it comes back unchanged — which is what makes
the shift safe to apply unconditionally. `quiet_hours_shifted` is stored, because "why is my post
at 08:00 when I said 23:00?" has to be answerable from the row rather than re-derived against a
window that may have been edited since.

On a spring-forward day the local time a window ends at may not exist. The conversion then lands
on the offset in force before the transition; one hour is the entire size of that error, so one
correction resolves it and a second failure is `PLANNER_QUIET_HOURS_UNRESOLVED` rather than a
search. Türkiye has no DST, which is exactly why `Europe/Berlin` is in the test suite: a planner
that assumed the tenant's offset never moves would pass every test written against Istanbul.

An unresolvable timezone is refused rather than defaulted to UTC. Silently defaulting would
publish a Turkish café's evening post three hours early and tell nobody.

## Idempotency

Two independent mechanisms, and neither depends on the planner remembering to check:

1. **The natural key.** `(subscription_item_id, period_start)` is unique. One standing demand
   produces one obligation per window, forever — including for windows whose obligation was later
   cancelled or expired, which must not be resurrected on the next tick.
2. **The tenant advisory lock.** `pg_advisory_xact_lock` in the planner's own namespace
   (`2_0022`, deliberately not entitlement's) serialises the read-then-insert sequence. It is
   taken *before* the existing period starts are read; taken afterwards it would serialise two
   runs that had both already decided to insert the same window.

The lock ordering across modules is fixed: dispatch takes the planner's lock and then, inside
`create_project`, entitlement takes its own — always that way round, so the two cannot form a
cycle.

Conversion carries its own key, derived from the obligation (`obligation:{id}:generation`). A
crash between "the project was created" and "the obligation was updated" therefore replays the
same project instead of buying a second one.

## Durability

Each of the three drains follows the discipline AGENTS.md requires of background work, and neither
the standing demand nor the obligation gets a paired `jobs` row — for the reason `content_projects`
has none: a planner's state *is* its result, and two rows saying it is two rows that can disagree
after a crash.

| | Status | Lease / due time | Attempts | Dead letter |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| planning | `planner_subscription_items.status` | `next_plan_at` | — (idempotent by key) | paused item |
| conversion | `content_obligations.status` | `next_attempt_at` | `attempts` | `blocked` + `reason_code` |
| scheduling | the project's own `state` | — (claim is a scan) | — | project stays `approved` |

`ck_content_obligation_due_matches_status` and the partial index `ix_content_obligations_due` are
written over the **same set** (`planned`, `blocked`), so the constraint and the claim cannot
disagree. `in_progress` is not terminal and still carries no due time: once an obligation has
become a project, that project is the durable job, and polling the queue entry as well would be a
second clock over the same work.

**All three drains run on beat ticks alone in this slice.** For planning that is principled —
nothing emits "a new period began", and a tick is the only thing that can observe the arrival of a
date, which is the same reason `content.pending.sweep` and `content.project.sweep` have no
producer. For conversion and scheduling it is not: both could be woken by an outbox event, and
`infrastructure/celery_publisher.py` was outside slice 2G's declared files. The cost is latency
rather than correctness, so both intervals are short (60 s and 120 s) and a unit test records the
claim so the next owner of that file finds it.

## The subscription item is a stand-in

PRD §12.2's real subscription item — plan, quality tier, billing — is Phase 3, blocked behind K1.
What §13.1 needs from it is much smaller: something that says "this business wants one Instagram
Reel a day, about this product, with this call to action". `planner_subscription_items` is exactly
that and nothing else, created by hand exactly as W20 created credit by hand.

The obligation keeps §13.1's own column name (`subscription_item_id`), so Phase 3 re-points a
foreign key rather than renaming a field the PRD names.

Two consequences worth knowing:

- The item names the **product and CTA**; the planner does not choose them. §13.2/6 ("aynı ürünün
  fazla tekrarlanmaması") is therefore a ranking penalty over recently used products, not a
  selection rule — which is what §13.2 asks for, since the section orders candidates rather than
  inventing them.
- The item names its **source assets**. §13.2/5 (media sufficiency) counts how many of them are
  renderable right now, and §14.1's fallback is "görevi beklet": an obligation with no usable
  footage ranks last and waits inside its window, rather than being refused or producing something
  worse.

## Authorization

Configuring the planner is `business.update`; reading it is `business.read`. That is PRD §4's
line, and it lands on the other side from `content`'s: saying what the business will publish and
when it is willing to publish is a business setting in the same sense a rename or a timezone is,
so an editor — who produces content and changes nothing about the business — cannot rewrite the
schedule the whole tenant runs on.

There is deliberately no permission called `planner.generate`. What the planner *does* with the
configuration is produce content, and that happens through `create_project` with its own
`content.generate` check, acting as the person who set the standing demand up. A member who loses
that permission stops producing content, which is the right answer for a background job with no
owner — and `audit_logs` keeps the same rule W20 set: a row written in the background still names
the person whose work moved.

## What this slice does not do

- **Publishing.** `PUBLISHING` and everything after it is Phase 4. `SCHEDULED` is where the
  machine stops.
- **Subscriptions.** Plans, quality tiers and billing are Phase 3 (K1).
- **§13.2/4 and §13.2/10.** Field open, rule absent, tested as such — see above.
- **Product or scene selection.** The standing demand names both; a cleverer selector wants the
  embeddings PRD §16.4–16.5 still lists as a Phase 1 gap.
